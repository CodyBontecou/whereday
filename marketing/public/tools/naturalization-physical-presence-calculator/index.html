Three numbers for your N-400: days present, absences that matter and the earliest filing date

List your trips abroad and this calculator gives you three results, each with its working and a link to the USCIS or eCFR source: your physical presence in the statutory window (at least 913 days on the general 5-year path), any absence that breaks or risks breaking continuous residence, and your earliest filing date under the 90-day early filing rule. Choose the 5-year path or the 3-year path for spouses of US citizens. Nothing is uploaded.

Calculate physical presence and continuous residence

This is a count, not legal advice; USCIS decides. It applies the counting rules below to the dates you enter. Missing or wrong trips change every result. It does not assess good moral character, the marital union test (see Policy Manual Vol. 12, Part G, Ch. 2), military or other special paths, Form N-470 or the 3-month state residence.     How USCIS counts days

    USCIS counts the day you depart and the day you return as days of physical presence (Policy Manual Vol. 12, Part D, Ch. 4). For continuous residence, USCIS does not count the dates of travel among the dates spent outside the United States (Ch. 3). Both rules point the same way: only the full days between departure and return are days away. This tool shows the two views side by side.

    The tool measures the length of each absence from your unclipped dates, and the days inside the window after clipping. 6 months and 1 year are calendar periods from the departure date: Ch. 3’s own example of leaving 1 January 2010 and returning 2 January 2011 is exactly 1 year. Leap years are handled by the calendar, not by a fixed 365 days.

    The window is the 3 or 5 years ending on the filing date, both ends included. The requirement is at least 30 months (913 days) on the 5-year path (Ch. 4) and 18 months on the 3-year path (8 CFR 319.1). USCIS gives no day count for 18 months, so the tool counts the days from 18 months before the filing date to the filing date, and shows that number.

    Absences of 6 to 12 months and of 1 year or more

    More than 6 months and less than 1 year: an absence is presumed to break the continuity of residence. You can rebut the presumption with evidence. Ch. 3 says evidence may include, but is not limited to, documentation that during the absence you did not terminate your US employment, kept your immediate family in the US, or retained full access to, or continued to own or lease, a home in the US. This tool only flags the trip; it cannot judge the evidence.

    1 year or more: the absence breaks continuous residence automatically, unless you have an approved Form N-470 (Application to Preserve Residence for Naturalization Purposes). On the 5-year path, Ch. 3 says you must then wait at least 4 years and 1 day after returning, and still rebut the presumption, because the absence is within the past 5 years. If you apply at least 4 years and 6 months after re-establishing residence, the 6-month presumption no longer applies. The tool shows both dates from your return date for the general path.

    Two caveats: the tool does not model N-470, and a break found in one absence does not tell you what other rules, such as abandonment of residence, would decide.

    The 90-day early filing rule

    You may file up to 90 days before you first meet the required 5-year period (or 3-year period for spouses). The rule is in INA 334 and 8 CFR 334.2(b), and Policy Manual Vol. 12, Part D, Ch. 6 explains it. The tool takes your resident-since date, adds 5 (or 3) years and subtracts 90 days.

    Filing early does not make you eligible sooner: you must still complete the residence period. Physical presence still has to add up, and the 3-month state residence is a separate check. Ch. 6 says that when the three months overlap with early filing, jurisdiction is based on the three months before the examination. This tool does not compute state residence.

    Where to get your trip dates

    Form N-400 asks about trips outside the United States (see the Form N-400 page). Find your travel history explains how to get your CBP I-94 history, and how to check it against passports and booking records. Keeping your own record as you travel avoids rebuilding it from memory later.

    The substantial presence test calculator is for a different question: the IRS tax test has its own 183-day weighted formula and is unrelated to these immigration counts. To count days in each country, use the days-in-country calculator, and see how Whereday records country days.

    What this does not cover

    • Good moral character.
    • The marital union test for the 3-year path, beyond this reminder that it exists (Policy Manual Vol. 12, Part G, Ch. 2).
    • Military and other special paths, and Form N-470.
    • The 3-month state residence, which is named here and not computed.

    Sources for the counting rules: Ch. 3, Ch. 4, Ch. 6 and 8 CFR 316.2, 316.5, 319.1 and 334.2.
